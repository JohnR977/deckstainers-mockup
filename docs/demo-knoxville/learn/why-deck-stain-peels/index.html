Why deck stain peels (and how to stop it)

Peeling is the number one complaint we hear. It's almost never bad luck. There are a few causes, and they're all fixable.

1. A film finish on a walking surface

Paints and solid stains form a film on top of the wood. The Forest Products Lab says film-forming finishes on decks are prone to trap moisture and fail by peeling1. Semi-transparent stains, by comparison, won't blister or peel even if water gets into the wood1. They fade instead.

2. The wood was wet

Stain on damp wood can't grip. That happens when someone stains the day after a rain, the morning after washing, or on brand-new lumber that hasn't dried. The lab recommends at least two warm, windy days of drying after cleaning1.

3. New coat on top of a failing coat

If the old finish is lifting, a new coat on top is only as strong as what's underneath. It peels right along with it. This is the most common mistake we fix.

4. Too much stain

More isn't better. Penetrating stain should soak in. The extra that sits in puddles on top dries into a soft film that scuffs and flakes. We back-brush and wipe off any excess before it dries.

5. Damaged surface from power washing

A high-pressure wash can tear up soft wood. The lab warns that aggressive scrubbing or power washing can remove wood from the surface1. That fuzzy surface takes stain unevenly. We wash with low pressure and the right cleaner, and let the chemistry do the work.

The fix

  1. Strip what's failing, all of it, down to sound wood.
  2. Brighten the wood so it's open and even.
  3. Let it dry fully.
  4. Apply a penetrating stain and back-brush it in.
  5. Recoat on schedule, before it wears through.

Stripping is the most work we do, so a peeling deck costs more than one that's just faded. Our price calculator shows the difference when you choose "Peeling."
